Why most positioning work fails before the first slide

Almost every brand brief I've read starts the same way: a list of what the company does, followed by a list of adjectives it would like to be associated with. Trustworthy. Innovative. Customer-first. None of that is positioning — it's a description, and a generic one, because every competitor's brief says roughly the same thing.

Positioning fails early because it usually gets treated as a writing problem: find better words for what we already believe about ourselves. It's actually a research problem. Who specifically is choosing you over the next option, and why that one reason and not the others you'd prefer they cared about? That's the entire structure behind the Positioning Audit: a competitive scan, an audience read built from evidence instead of assumption, and a messaging gap analysis that shows exactly where the current story contradicts itself.

What a flagship launch teaches you about a five-person startup

The ARKET flagship in Milan wasn't complicated because the product was complicated — it was complicated because hundreds of small decisions had to resolve the same way, hundreds of times a day, across a team that had never worked together before. A five-person startup has the same root problem in miniature: the founder knows exactly how to talk about the business, but the second hire doesn't, and neither will the freelancer writing next month's ad copy.

The lesson that actually transfers isn't the scale, it's the discipline: decide the small stuff on purpose, once, in a form someone else can pick up and use correctly without you in the room. That's what a brand voice guide is for — the least glamorous document in any of the three packages, and usually the one clients come back to most.

The case for auditing yourself in public

Most new agencies fill their portfolio page with mockups — logo concepts for a fictional coffee shop, a rebrand nobody asked for. I get why: there's no client history yet, and an empty page looks worse than a fake one. But a mockup can't be wrong. Nobody can check it against reality, which means it doesn't actually demonstrate judgment — just taste.

The Socialbooth piece exists instead of a mockup: a real audit of a real, currently-operating company's actual public brand, done with full disclosure and a promise to take it down if anyone objects. That's the bar for anything on this page going forward: real companies, real findings, checkable claims, clearly marked as unsolicited where that's what they are.
